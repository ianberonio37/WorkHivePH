Introduction to Lock-Out Tag-Out (LOTO) Procedures

LOTO stands for lock-out tag-out. It keeps a machine from starting while someone works on it. Done right, it prevents serious injury and death.

In the Philippines, LOTO is the law, not advice. RA 11058 makes the Occupational Safety and Health Standards enforceable. Its rules are DOLE Department Order 252, Series of 2025. That order replaced Department Order 198-18 on 16 May 2025. They require safe work procedures for hazardous work. A written LOTO procedure is one of them.

The fine grows with each day the violation stands. The OSH law sets it, not the inspector.

A written procedure lets a crew who did not plan the job isolate the machine as the planner meant. It is also the only thing an inspector can read after the fact. The procedure says what should happen. The dated record says what did.

WorkHive's logbook carries a LOTO field on each job record. So each isolation has a date and a name on it.

What a LOTO procedure has to contain

Write a LOTO procedure per asset, not per plant. The night crew did not plan the job, yet they must isolate the machine the same way. So the procedure names the actual disconnect and the actual valve. It also names the energy left in the machine once you shut both.

Every per-asset procedure names the same eight things:

  1. the asset and its tag number
  2. every energy source it carries
  3. the exact isolating device for each one
  4. where stored energy sits, and how to release it
  5. how to verify zero energy, and with what instrument
  6. who may authorise the isolation
  7. who holds the locks
  8. the steps for putting it back

Write those, and the sequence below is you walking your own document. Plants that run around the clock often split the job. One crew applies the isolation and another releases it. So write it for someone who was not there when the lock went on.

The 7-Step Lockout Sequence

The order is the safety measure. Each step relies on the ones before it. Verification comes last because it proves the other six worked. Run them in this order every time:

  1. Prepare. Find every energy source the job will expose, not just the obvious one. Think of electrical supply, hydraulics, compressed air, steam and process fluid. Add springs under tension and loads that gravity holds up. Add anything still hot. Write them down before you touch the machine.
  2. Notify. Tell operations and everyone on or near the machine that it is going down. Tell them when to expect it back.
  3. Shut down. Stop the equipment the normal way, with its own controls. Do not stop it by pulling its isolator.
  4. Isolate. Operate each isolating device you listed in step 1. Open the disconnect, close and chain the valve, and disconnect the line.
  5. Lock and tag. Each person doing the work puts on their own lock and tag. Only that person removes it. On a multi-trade job, one hasp holds several locks. The machine cannot start while any lock is still on.
  6. Release stored energy. Bleed the lines and drain the casing. Discharge the capacitors, block or lower the raised part, and let it cool. Isolating a source does not empty what is already downstream of it. This is what kills people who did everything else right.
  7. Verify zero energy, last, at the point of work. Try to start the machine with its normal control, then return the control to off. Then test: put a meter on the conductors, check the gauge reads zero, and open the bleed valve. Verification is a measurement, not an assumption.

Why the last two are in that order. Verify before the release, and the reading goes stale. It describes a machine that is gone by the time you open it. A pressurised accumulator stays live. So does a charged drive capacitor, or a load hanging in the air. They stay live even after a clean verification. Release first, verify second. Then the reading you trust is the state you work in.

Preparation is paperwork, and doing it well makes the rest possible. For Pump P-204B, settle one fact before anyone walks to it. The pump has two energy sources, not one. One is the 480V supply at its disconnect. The other is the process water still in the casing and the suction line.

Picture a procedure that lists only the electrical supply. A crew can follow it to the letter and still open a flooded casing.

The middle of the sequence is where procedures quietly go wrong. A machine that has stopped looks finished. It is not. A pump that has coasted to a halt still has 480V on it. "It stopped turning" is not a zero-energy check. The only check that counts is a try-start, then an instrument reading at the point of work.

Between those two sits the step that is easiest to skip and worst to skip. Drain the casing, bleed the line and discharge the drive. Inspectors look for any gap between the plan and the work. So does every incident investigation, first.

Releasing the isolation is its own procedure, not step 8. Before any lock comes off, check four things. The job is over and every tool is out of the machine. The guards are back on. Everyone is clear, and you have told them the power is coming back.

Then each worker removes their own lock. Nobody removes a lock for an absent colleague. That shortcut kills people on the next shift. Sometimes a lock must come off while its owner is at home. Then the supervisor contacts the owner first, decides, and writes it down. It is never bolt cutters and a judgement call.

Sample Equipment-Specific LOTO Procedure

Here is a sample for a 480V pump, P-204B, in the Laguna Technopark. The plant runs around the clock, so an isolation can outlive the crew that applied it. Copy the shape, not the values. The energy sources change from asset to asset.

The pump has two energy sources: the 480V supply and the water pressure in its lines. The supervisor or shift in-charge checks that the planner listed them both.

  1. Notify operations, and get the plant supervisor's approval.
  2. Shut down the pump with its own control.
  3. Isolate the supply at the lockable disconnect switch.
  4. Lock and tag it, one lock per worker.
  5. Drain the water from the pump and its piping.
  6. Verify, and only now. Try-start from the local control. Meter the conductors at the motor terminals. Confirm the drain runs dry.
  7. Record it on the job's logbook entry, with the permit number.

An inspector reads the written procedure against what the crew does. So a procedure copied from another plant and never walked is worse than none. It documents a sequence nobody follows.

When the job ends, release runs in reverse, person by person. Tools out, guards on, everyone clear and told. Each worker pulls their own lock. Then close the disconnect and hand the pump back to operations.

Sign-off and the written record

An isolation nobody recorded is one you cannot prove. On a three-shift plant, the next crew cannot read it either. So two things must be true. The lock on the machine is physical and personal. And someone writes the isolation down, with a date.

What WorkHive records, exactly. The logbook entry for the job has a LOTO field. It is a checkbox that says you applied energy isolation. An optional permit-to-work number sits beside it. Tick it, and the entry shows a LOTO badge in the logbook list. The badge carries the permit number if you gave one.

The field sits on the entry, so it carries the entry's date and the writer's name. You can also search and count it. Before this field, you could only find an isolation by reading free-text notes. So you found it by luck and could not count it.

What it does not do, so you can plan for it. It is one field on the job record, not a lockout workflow. It does not walk the crew through the seven steps. It does not hold a supervisor's approval before work starts. It does not track each lock on and off. Those stay on your permit paperwork and on the hasp.

What the digital record adds is the part paper loses. It is a dated, named, searchable trail an inspector can read months later. Under RA 11058 and DO 252-25, that is the part you must produce.

The LOTO procedure template

Here is the template. Copy it into your own document and fill one in per asset. Keep it where the crew doing the job can reach it. A procedure filed in the office is not a procedure.

  • Asset and tag number: the name the crew uses, not the one on the purchase order.
  • Energy sources: one line each, with type, where it enters and its size. For example: 480V 3-phase; process water at line pressure; 6 bar instrument air.
  • Isolating device: for each source, the exact disconnect, valve or breaker. Give its tag and where it is.
  • Stored energy: what stays in the machine after isolation. Add the drain, bleed, discharge or blocking step that empties it.
  • Verification: what you try-start and what you measure. Name the instrument and the reading that means dead.
  • Authorisation and locks: who authorises, by role, and who holds the locks. Say how many locks the hasp must take.
  • Release steps: the checks before power comes back, and who removes which lock.
  • Revision date: and who walked it last. A procedure nobody walked against the real machine is a draft.

Two habits decide whether a crew uses a template or files it. Write it against the real machine, not from memory: walk to the disconnect and read its tag. And keep it short enough that the crew reads it at the machine.

Procedures go stale as machines change. Re-walk each one when the asset changes, and on a fixed interval otherwise. Date each revision.

Your logbook's recorded isolations show which procedures the crew actually uses. Some assets have a written procedure but no recorded isolation. Look into those. Either no one maintains them, or no one records the work.

A LOTO programme costs three things: training time, the locks and tags, and the hours to write each procedure. Plants underestimate the third. The template above cuts it. You fill in eight fields per asset instead of starting from a blank page.

Frequently asked questions

What is the purpose of LOTO procedures?
The purpose of LOTO procedures is to ensure the safety of workers during maintenance activities by preventing accidental start-up of equipment.
Which Philippine rules actually require LOTO?
RA 11058 and its implementing rules, now DOLE Department Order 252-25 (in force since 16 May 2025, succeeding DO 198-18), make compliance with the Occupational Safety and Health Standards mandatory and set the penalties, and they require safe work procedures for hazardous work: which is what a written LOTO procedure is. Plants certified to ISO 45001 also carry it as an operational-control requirement.
What is the 7-step lockout sequence?
Prepare, notify, shut down, isolate, lock and tag, release stored energy, then verify zero energy. Verification is last on purpose: it is the step that proves the other six worked, so doing it before stored energy is released tells you nothing about the machine you are about to open.
How do I create a LOTO procedure for my equipment?
To create a LOTO procedure for your equipment, you can use the template provided in this article and tailor it to your specific equipment needs.
What is the importance of sign-off requirements in LOTO procedures?
Sign-off requirements are important in LOTO procedures to ensure that all personnel involved in the maintenance activity acknowledge that the equipment is safe to work on.
Where does WorkHive record that an isolation happened?
On the logbook entry for the job. It carries a LOTO checkbox and an optional permit-to-work reference, so the isolation is dated, attributed to the worker who recorded it, and searchable later. It records that an isolation happened: it is not a lockout workflow and does not replace your permit paperwork or the locks themselves.

Keep your LOTO permits in a dated audit trail. Each one carries a date and a name. A DOLE inspector then sees a record, not a memory.
