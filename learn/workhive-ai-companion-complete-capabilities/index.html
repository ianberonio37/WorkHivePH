Meet the two companions: Hezekiah and Zaniah

The companion is two experts in one. Pick the one that fits your question, and switch between them with one tap.

Hezekiah is the hands-on fix expert. Picture a warm senior Filipino technician at the machine with you. He leads with the next move, not a lecture. His world is this machine, this shift, this breakdown. Ask him for a torque value or the lock-out order. He also covers lube intervals, safety gear and the next check.

Zaniah is the planner. Picture a calm Filipino operations planner who sees the whole hive. She spots the patterns a busy foreman misses. Her world is this week, this hive, this trend.

She leads with the pattern and weighs cost, risk and timing. She pushes for escalation when the numbers back it. Everyone starts with her, because orientation comes before torque values.

Each one points you to the other when your question crosses over. Ask Hezekiah if a fault is becoming a pattern, and he sends you to Zaniah. Ask Zaniah what torque to set, and she sends you to Hezekiah. One tap switches, so you are never stuck with the wrong expert.

Wherever you are in WorkHive, you will see a pulsing round button. It is on almost every page of the app. Tap it and a chat panel opens, with an "Ask anything" box and a microphone. It already knows which page you are on. There is also a full My Work Assistant page and a private Voice Journal.

The floating helper and the Work Assistant page use the same two personas. Each keeps its own memory of your chats. They also check numbers in different ways, as the next section explains. For more on both of them, see our guide on Hezekiah and Zaniah.

It answers from your own records, and will not pass off an invented number

This is what makes the companion safe to trust on a plant floor. It does not answer from generic internet advice. It answers from your own hive's records. That means your logbook, machines, stock, schedules, projects and risks.

Ask a broad question like "how's my plant?", and you get no textbook paragraph. It puts your alerts, PM status, low stock, projects and top risks into one answer. In one live test, a single reply covered run time, time between breakdowns and PM completion. It also named four low parts and the machine most at risk.

It also names its source on how-to questions. Ask how to fix something, and it searches your hive's fault history and PM notes. It cites the record it used, so you can check it yourself.

And it is built not to pass off an invented number as yours. Two different checks do this, one for each surface.

On the floating helper, a check reads every reply. It removes any sentence with a number it cannot trace. A number must come from your records, from you, or from the benchmark table. If what is left reads badly, you get an honest pointer instead of a fragment.

On the My Work Assistant page, the rule is gentler. Deleting a real figure would be its own kind of wrong. It keeps an untraced percentage, but calls it a rough guess. It is never presented as a figure from your records.

The practical rule: if it calls a number yours, it is yours. If it lacks the answer here, it says so. Then it points you to the right page. It will not invent a machine tag either. Name a machine that is not in your list, and it tells you so.

Why this matters: a helper that makes up a torque value or a stock count is worse than none. You might act on it. The companion is built to catch that before you see it. If a number is on your screen as yours, it came from your data.

Talk in your own language, and it reads the answer back out loud

You do not have to type, and you do not have to speak English. Tap the companion's mic and just talk. Use English, Taglish, Bisaya, Ilocano or more. It understands eight Philippine languages. It works out which one you used and writes down what you said. Then it replies in English. Just talk the way you talk on the floor.

There are usage caps behind the scenes. They stop one account from running up the bill for everyone, and they are not there to ration a shift. An ordinary day of asking and dictating will not reach them.

It also reads the reply out loud in a Filipino English voice. So you can keep both hands on the job and listen, instead of stopping to read a screen. That is the whole point of a hands-free helper at the machine. For more on speaking instead of typing, see voice to text on the plant floor.

One spoken sentence fills a whole work order

Filling out a logbook entry by hand is the chore everyone skips when they are busy. That is how history gets lost. Voice turns it into ten seconds of talking, in two ways.

On the Logbook, tap Speak to Fill and describe what happened in one plain sentence. It listens in Philippine English, in Chrome on Android. It fills in the problem, likely cause, action, type, status and downtime. It also flags any parts you named, any lock-off and a note for your supervisor.

From the companion's mic on any page, speak an action like "I just replaced the V-belt on Pump P-5". That works in any of the eight languages. The right page opens with a confirmation chip, so you can check it and apply it.

Neither one writes to your records on its own. You see the draft first and fix anything that is off. Nothing saves until you confirm, so your approval and your record trail stay intact.

Ask about one machine and get its whole history

When you troubleshoot one machine, you do not want the plant average. You want everything about that machine. On the Asset Hub, ask about any machine by name. The companion pulls its whole story together:

  • The machine itself and the parent equipment it belongs to
  • Its recent logbook entries and the PM work done, including PMs that were due and skipped on purpose
  • How long it usually runs between breakdowns, and what drives its current risk score
  • Failures on sister machines of the same type, since a fault new to this pump may be in another logbook
  • Its reliability work, checked by an engineer. That covers failure modes, strategy, the wear-out curve and when to inspect

So ask what keeps happening to Pump P-203, and whether it hit the others. The answer comes from that machine's real history. Ask it for a part number or next week's schedule, and it says it does not have that here. Parts live on the Inventory page, and upcoming jobs on the PM Scheduler. We go deeper in the guide to one machine's full history.

It remembers what you told it, even days later

The companion keeps a memory. It holds the last part of your chat, a running summary, and the important things you told it. That memory lasts across turns, across pages and across days.

Come back tomorrow and ask what you told it about Pump P-203 last week. It quotes your own words back, not a guess. A chat you start on one page carries on when you open the companion on another page.

It treats memory as carefully as data. It recalls the exact thing you asked about. It will not swap in some other number it remembers. It never restates an old chat as the current confirmed value. If it is not sure, it says so.

It plans your shift and drafts your paperwork

The companion does not only answer questions. It also does the planning work that eats a supervisor's morning.

On Shift Brain, the plan builds the first time someone opens the page in a shift. A supervisor can also tap Re-run plan. The plan lists the top risks and the PM jobs due. It adds leftover work, the parts to pre-stage and a short briefing.

Other pages use it for the wider picture. Analytics gives a next-failure forecast and a parts stock-out outlook. On the Hive Board, the Reliability Coach plans a supervisor's week. All of it comes from your real data, and it will not invent a value to fill a gap. Read more in autonomous shift planning.

It also drafts your paperwork. Ask it to write a note to your boss, a lessons note, a handover or a report cover. It writes the draft for you. It cannot send the message itself, so it hands you a ready draft to send.

It points you to the right page, and keeps you safe

WorkHive is a big platform, and the companion is also your guide through it. Voice actions have safety brakes. Leave the machine name blank, or name a machine that does not exist, and it stops at a confirmation step. It refuses a destructive or bulk request like "delete all the machines".

On safety, the companion follows Philippine rules, not foreign codes. Its safety answers follow RA 11058, the law on workplace safety. Electrical answers follow the Philippine Electrical Code. It walks you through the safe steps: isolate the energy, lock it, tag it, and check for zero energy. Then it reminds you to use a qualified person and get the permit.

It refuses to suggest an unsafe shortcut. It will not make destructive or bulk changes on its own. Those belong with your supervisor, on the record.

It is honest about its own limits. It cannot place an order, send an email or text, make a call, pay anyone or control equipment. It tells you so and offers the real option, usually a draft or the right page. It also tells the truth about WorkHive. It is free, with no paid tiers, and it works beside your existing systems.

Frequently asked questions

What is the WorkHive AI Companion?
It is a free helper built into WorkHive that you talk to like a workmate who knows the plant. It comes as two experts you switch with one tap: Hezekiah, the hands-on fix expert, and Zaniah, the planner who spots patterns across the team. What is the difference between Hezekiah and Zaniah?
Hezekiah is the hands-on fix expert: torque values, the lock-out order, lube intervals, protective gear and the next check on the machine you are standing at. Zaniah is the planner: she looks across the whole team and the whole week, spots repeating problems, weighs cost, risk and timing, and helps you plan and escalate. You switch between them with one tap. When your question crosses over, each one points you to the other. Zaniah is the default for everyone, because orientation usually comes before torque values.
Can the companion make up numbers or invent data?
Not as one of yours. On the floating helper, a built-in check removes any sentence whose number does not trace back to your records, a value you told it, or the standard benchmark table. On the My Work Assistant page the rule is gentler on purpose: a percentage it cannot trace stays, but it is marked as a rough estimate, never as a figure from your records. So a number offered as yours is yours, and an estimate is labelled as one. It will not invent a machine tag that is not in your list, and it only recalls what you actually told it. If it lacks the answer here, it says so. Then it points you to the right page.

Which safety rules does the companion follow?

It walks you through the safe steps (isolate, lock, tag, check for zero energy, use a qualified person, get the permit) and it refuses to suggest an unsafe shortcut.
