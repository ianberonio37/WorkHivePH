## Why every maintenance team needs a skill matrix

Build it as a grid of every worker against every skill you need. Score each cell on WorkHive's 5-level scale. Level 1 is Safety Awareness. Level 2 is Supervised Practice. Level 3 is Independent Technician.

Level 4 is Senior / Specialist. Level 5 is Engineer / Strategist. Assess each cell with the 3-input rule. Check certificates, logbook history and a hands-on test. A Level 4 or 5 signs off the test.

Without a skill matrix, three patterns repeat in every Philippine plant:

- **Work goes to whoever is free, not whoever can do it.** On the night shift, an electrical fault goes to the mechanic. Nobody else is on. The fix half-works; the asset is back in failure 3 weeks later.
- **Senior technicians do junior work.** The most skilled person on the floor changes a lightbulb because no one delegated it. Plant pays senior-rate for junior tasks.
- **Promotion decisions are political, not earned.** Two technicians want the senior role. The one who "gets along" with the supervisor gets it. The one with the better skill history gets bypassed and leaves the plant.

A skill matrix fixes all three by making competence visible. No other HR document does more for a maintenance team.

## The 5-level competency scale

WorkHive uses a 5-level scale. Level 3 is the technician who can finally work alone. Level 4 is the plant's go-to senior. Level 5 is the engineer who sets the standard for the whole plant. That top level makes the matrix useful for assigning work and for career growth, not just pass or fail.

| Level | Name | What it means | What they can do |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Safety Awareness** | Knows the basics and the safety rules. Has done the task once or twice, closely watched | Help, observe, ask questions; cannot work alone |
| 2 | **Supervised Practice** | Can do the task with a buddy or under supervision; correct outcome most of the time | Pair work, scheduled jobs, light troubleshooting |
| 3 | **Independent Technician** | Does the task alone, safely and well. Can sort out most variations | Lead jobs, unsupervised work, on-call response |
| 4 | **Senior / Specialist** | The plant's reference for this skill; can train others and update the SOP | Training, assessment, SOP authorship, mentoring |
| 5 | **Engineer / Strategist** | Sets the standard for the discipline and improves it across the plant | Standards, planning, reliability strategy, cross-team improvement |

Empty cell = not assessed or not relevant for this technician. A blank is honest. A guessed level is dangerous.

## The disciplines that belong on the matrix

Start with what your assets actually demand, not a generic template. For a typical plant here, the list of disciplines looks like this:

- **Mechanical:** pumps, motors, fans and compressors. Vessels and heat exchangers. Pipefitting, alignment and bearing changes
- **Electrical:** low voltage up to 600V and medium voltage above 1kV. Motor controls, VFDs, switchgear, cabling and lighting
- **Instrumentation:** loop checks, and calibration for pressure, heat, flow and level. HART setup, valve positioners and control valve trim
- **HVAC and Refrigeration:** AHUs, FCUs and chillers. Refrigerant handling (TESDA NC II) and BMS programming
- **Civil and Structural:** foundations, structural steel, drainage, roofing, flooring repair
- **Safety:** LOTO (lock-out/tag-out), confined space, work at height and hot work. Permit-to-work roles and first aid
- **Cross-cutting (Level 3+ techs only):** root cause analysis (RCA). Also vibration and oil analysis, thermography and ultrasonic leak checks
- **Software and Digital (new in 2026):** the WorkHive Logbook and PM Scheduler. Prompting the AI work assistant, and basic Excel pivots

This typically gives 25 to 40 specific skills per discipline area. Keep it tight; matrices with 200+ skills become unmaintainable.

WorkHive rolls these into five disciplines. They are Mechanical, Electrical, Instrumentation, Facilities Management and Production Lines. That keeps the matrix easy to scan. Each discipline still covers its own skills underneath.

## Example skill matrix: 6 technicians, 5 disciplines

Here is a sample matrix for a small Philippine plant team. It uses the five WorkHive disciplines:

| Technician | Mechanical | Electrical | Instrumentation | Facilities Mgmt | Production Lines |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Cruz, R.** (Senior) | 4 | 3 | 2 | 3 | 3 |
| **Santos, M.** (Senior) | 3 | 4 | 4 | 2 | 2 |
| **Aquino, J.** (Tech 2) | 3 | 3 | 3 | 3 | 2 |
| **Reyes, P.** (Tech 2) | 2 | 3 | 2 | 3 | 1 |
| **Garcia, A.** (Tech 1) | 2 | 1 | 1 | 2 | 1 |
| **Dela Cruz, L.** (Apprentice) | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 | - |

Reading this matrix:

- **Coverage gaps:** Only Santos reaches Level 4 in Instrumentation. Only Santos and Aquino reach Level 3 or higher. If Santos is on leave, calibration and loop work leans on one person. Action: train Cruz from Level 2 to 3 on Instrumentation (priority).
- **Underutilised expertise:** Santos is Level 4 in both Electrical and Instrumentation. Nobody else reaches that level. Should be running training sessions for the others.
- **Promotion case:** Aquino is Level 3 in four of the five disciplines. The fifth is Level 2. Strong promotion case to senior tech.
- **Production Lines gap:** Only Cruz reaches Level 3 in Production Lines. The apprentice has no rating yet. Cross-training here reduces line-startup dependence on the two seniors.

## How to assess fairly (the 3-input rule)

Skill assessment is the part most plants get wrong. Either it is only the supervisor's opinion, which favours favourites. Or it is only self-rating, which rarely matches reality. The rule that works: each level needs three inputs behind it.

1. **Certificates and formal training records.** Think TESDA NC II for refrigeration. Or OEM training on one VFD line. Or in-house training on pump PMs. These are the floor; a technician without the cert cannot be above Level 2 in that skill.
2. **Demonstrated logbook history.** The technician has logged at least N entries in this discipline in the past 12 months. Each ended well: no rework, no escalation, no safety incident. In WorkHive you read this from the Logbook yourself. Logged work does not raise a skill level; you earn a level by passing the quiz.
3. **Practical assessment by a Level 4 instructor.** A planned 30-minute test where the technician does the task on a real or practice asset. The instructor signs off the level.

All three must agree. A technician with no cert but 50 logbook entries does not reach Level 3. They lack the formal grounding. A technician with the cert but no logbook history does not reach Level 3. They lack the proven work. A technician with both who fails the hands-on test does not reach Level 3. They lack the judgment.

## How to use the matrix for daily assignment

The planner lists the day's tasks and the level each one needs. Then the planner checks the matrix for who is on shift at that level. The planner picks a person, and the supervisor approves. WorkHive does not filter this for you.

The PM Scheduler holds no required skill level, and the Skill Matrix is personal. The one place it matches workers to assets is the daily AMC brief. Its crew builder pairs a worker with each risky asset.

Without the matrix, the planner just asks who is free. The plant pays the cost in rework, escalations, and unplanned downtime.

## Career protection in the AI era

This is the part worth saying out loud. In the AI era, technicians whose skill is on record stay protected. Those whose skill lives only in their heads get bypassed.

Here is the mechanism:

- **When management evaluates headcount cuts**, HR opens the skill matrix first. Plants keep their Level 3 and 4 techs in key disciplines. Technicians whose value lives only in their head are easy to cut on a spreadsheet.
- **Applying to work overseas?** Employers want proof of skill. Think Saudi, the UAE, Singapore or Japan. A Level 4 instructor's sign-off plus 200 logbook entries is proof they can check. "Trust me, I'm good" is not.
- **When the AI work assistant answers from plant data**, it answers out of the records your team wrote. It credits the record, not the person. But every logbook entry keeps its author's name. So you can find the technician whose root-cause work keeps showing up. Documented work is what makes that traceable at all.
- **When the plant gets sold or merged**, the new owner looks at the skill matrix to decide who to retain. Undocumented technicians get severance; documented ones get transition bonuses.

Document your work. Document your skills. Filipino plant workers need the skill matrix most, and use it least.

## Common mistakes

- **Building a 200-skill matrix in week one.** Start with 40 to 60 skills covering the assets you actually run. Add more annually.
- **Self-assessment alone.** Technicians average 1 level too high on self-assessment. The 3-input rule fixes this.
- **Updating once and never again.** Quarterly review minimum. Annually re-baseline against the current asset mix.
- **Sharing the matrix only with HR.** The supervisor needs it daily to assign work. The technician needs it monthly to see their gap. HR needs it yearly for promotion. All three audiences must have access.
- **Linking the matrix only to pay.** The matrix is for assignment, training, and career. If it becomes only a pay-grade input, technicians game the assessment.

**The bigger picture:** Philippine plants skip this tool more than any other. Plants spend millions on equipment and almost nothing on recording who can run it. Plants that build a working skill matrix gain a lot. They assign work better and cut MTTR. They plan training, defend promotions, and make workers' value visible.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a skill matrix and why do maintenance teams need one?

A skill matrix is a grid that lists every technician on one axis and every skill or competency required by the plant on the other, with each cell showing that person's certified level. Maintenance teams need it because work assignment without a skill matrix defaults to who is available, not who is qualified. The result is electrical work going to a mechanical technician, motor PMs being missed because no one is on-shift who is certified, and senior technicians being underused while juniors do work above their level.

### What competency scale should I use?

WorkHive uses a 5-level scale for industrial maintenance: Level 1 (Safety Awareness) means the technician understands the basics and safety rules but cannot work unsupervised. Level 2 (Supervised Practice) means they can do the task with supervision or a buddy. Level 3 (Independent Technician) means they can do the task alone safely and competently. Level 4 (Senior / Specialist) means they can train others and are the plant's reference for that skill. Level 5 (Engineer / Strategist) means they set the standard for the discipline and plan it across the plant. The extra top level is what separates a reliable senior from the person who improves the whole discipline.

### How should I assess a technician's level?

Three inputs in combination: (1) certificates and training records (TESDA, OEM courses, internal training), (2) demonstrated work history from the WorkHive Logbook (entries in that discipline with successful outcomes), and (3) a practical assessment by a Level 4 instructor. Avoid self-assessment alone; it correlates poorly with actual competence. Avoid supervisor-only assessment; supervisors tend to over-rate technicians they like.

### Which skills or disciplines should be on the matrix?

Start with the disciplines your assets actually need: mechanical (rotating equipment, static equipment, pipefitting), electrical (LV up to 600V, MV above 1kV, motor controls, VFD), instrumentation (loop tuning, calibration, transmitter sizing), HVAC (refrigeration, ducting, BMS), civil (structural, foundations, drainage), and safety (LOTO, confined space, work at height, permit to work). Then add cross-cutting skills like root cause analysis, vibration analysis, and thermography for technicians at Level 3+.

### How is the skill matrix used for daily assignment?

The planner filters available technicians by competency level required for the task. A motor electrical overhaul tagged as Level 3 Electrical 600V only shows technicians at that level or higher who are on shift. A routine pump greasing tagged Level 1 Mechanical Rotating shows anyone qualified. WorkHive does not filter this for you: the PM Scheduler has no required skill level, so the planner checks the matrix. The daily AMC brief is where WorkHive itself matches a worker to each risky asset.

### Can you use the skill matrix for promotion decisions?

Yes. The matrix gives an objective answer to who has earned the next role. A technician who has reached Level 3 in 4 of 5 disciplines has a documented case for promotion to senior tech. A technician at Level 1 across the board has a development plan. This is the strongest worker-side benefit of the skill matrix: in the AI era, the technicians whose competence is documented get protected; the ones whose competence is informal get bypassed when management cuts costs. The skill matrix is career insurance.
